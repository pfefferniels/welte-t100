Welte-Mignon T-100 · roll WR0225_02 · emulated pedal action

Träumerei, pedalled by a bellows

A red Welte roll does not tell the piano to hold the dampers up. It fires a valve, and a bellows then takes about a fifth of a second to lift them. This is that travel, derived from the punched code and written out as continuous MIDI instead of the on and off the usual converters emit.

What the continuous reading changes

The pedal is pressed 45 times and lifted 46, the median press lasting 1.05 s and the median lift 1.44 s. Read as a switch that is 91 instants; read as a bellows it is 91 traversals.

Over the whole roll the dampers are somewhere between the strings and their full lift for 11.8 of the 166 seconds — 7.1 per cent of it, or 129 ms of travel at every one of the 91 changes. A converter that transmits the latch puts all of it at a single instant.

Only once does a change fail to finish. The furthest short is at 2:33.8, where the roll cancels the pedal and takes it again 60 ms later. The dampers get 3 per cent of the way back down before the next command reverses them. That is where this mechanism holds a position of its own, and it is what Hagmann guessed at — half-pedal effects “durch Ueberlagerung der verschiedenen vom Notenband ausgehenden Befehle”.

Where the numbers come from

The mechanism is Hagmann’s, from Anhang 16 and pp. 106–107 of his 1984 dissertation. A momentary punch on line 93 sets a latching relay; its output reaches membrane chamber 12 through throttle 11, which is what delays the start; the double valve 14 then seals the atmospheric bore 15 and opens conduit 16, and blower vacuum closes bellows 18. Releasing it runs the same delay and then bleeds air back through throttle 17. Welte’s own scale-roll instructions send the technician to exactly those two adjusters: control 9c to 11 if the dampers rise too slowly, control 9b to 17 if they fall too slowly.

The travel follows the same flow law the emulator fits to the Nuancierbälge, since it is the same kind of device — a bellows filling through a conduit, moving at a speed that depends on how far it still has to go.

What this does not show

None of the travel times is measured. Hagmann gives none, and the pedals leave no drawn line on any roll, so there is nothing to fit them against. They are argued from the adjusters and bounded by the shortest change the roll asks for. A real T-100 in playing order would settle them in an afternoon.

The shape of the ramp is far better supported than its length. That a bellows takes time and arrives gradually follows from the mechanism. How long is a guess within a factor of two, and every percentage on this page moves with it.

Hagmann is explicit that there is no depth control — “die Dämpfer werden stets in derselben Geschwindigkeit und immer vollständig von den Saiten abgehoben” (p. 112). Every intermediate position here is one the mechanism is passing through, never one it was told to hold.

This is the apparatus, not the performance. What the recording pianist’s foot did, and how faithfully the punching represents it, is a separate question that nothing here bears on.

CC 64 is a switch at 64 in General MIDI. Renderers that model a real damper read the whole range; many samplers do not, and will collapse all of this back to on and off.
